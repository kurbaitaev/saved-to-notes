### Slide 2
*Slide with a line graph showing emotional arc from HOPEFUL at the beginning curving down through a turning point to DEFLATED at the ending.*

Scene Polarity

Beginning: HOPEFUL — Ending: DEFLATED
turning point

A scene should usually shift the emotional or story value in some way. If the character enters hopeful and leaves equally hopeful (or distressed), with no new complication, decision, or understanding, it miiiight feel static.

### Slide 3
*Nested concentric box diagram illustrating four levels of narrative proximity, from distant narration (outermost pink) to raw experience (innermost olive dot).*

Psychic Distance

[nested boxes, outermost to innermost]: distant narration / character observation / direct thought / raw experience

This is about how close the narration sits to the character's mind. Moving closer can intensify vulnerable or high-stakes moments. Pulling back can speed through time, give context, or stop every moment from feeling equally intense.

### Slide 4
*Three-circle Venn diagram in muted green and mauve with READER, HERO, and VILLAIN as the three sets and 'shared' labeled at the center overlap.*

Information Architecture

[Venn diagram: READER / HERO / VILLAIN — 'shared' at center]

This is the deliberate control of who knows what, when they learn it, and how they interpret it. The reader knowing more can create dread, the character knowing more can create intrigue, and different characters holding different pieces can create conflict.

### Slide 5
*Two-row comparison diagram: top row (STRONGER) chains event to meaning to choice to consequence in green; bottom row (WEAKER) chains four identical event boxes in mauve.*

Emotional Causality

How it actually works (STRONGER):
event → meaning → choice → consequence

How it often works (WEAKER):
event → event → event → event

I know this sucks but a story can be logically plotted and still feel emotionally disconnected. Events should not only happen one after another. Each event should change how the character sees the situation, and that emotional shift should influence what they do next.

Readers can only hold so much unfamiliar info at once. When several new characters, locations, magical terms, and political relationships appear together, too much is competing for attention in the same moment. Introduce information according to what the reader needs now, not everything you know.

### Slide 7
*Side-by-side diagram: a dotted-line travel route (3 weeks) given 3 pages on the left vs a doorstep confrontation between two figures (30 seconds) also given 3 pages on the right.*

Compression vs Expansion

3 weeks on the road → 3 pages
30 seconds on the door → 3 pages

Page time tells the reader what matters. Always a good idea to expand moments involving irreversible choices, emotional rupture, discovery, or danger. Buut compress travel, routine, and anything the reader can easily infer. Pacing is often about giving the most important moments enough room to land.
