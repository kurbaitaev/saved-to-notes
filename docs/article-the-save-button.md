# The save button is not a memory

You are scrolling. Something good goes past. A book you should read, a founder
saying the thing you have been trying to articulate for a month, a workout that
looks like it would actually work. You tap the bookmark icon. There is a small
satisfying click, the icon fills in, and you keep scrolling.

Nothing about that felt like a loss. It felt like the opposite. You caught
something.

Now go and open your saved folder. Not later. Now. Scroll to the bottom, to the
things you saved in spring, and ask yourself, honestly, which of them you can
describe without opening.

I did this and it was worse than I expected.

## The number

In 2021 three researchers ran the experiment properly. Ofer Bergman, Steve
Whittaker and Jonathan Schooler gave fifty people retrieval tasks, twenty-one
each, five of which pointed at pages those people had personally bookmarked.
Two hundred and fifty bookmarked targets in total.

Forty-one of them were retrieved using the bookmark. Sixteen percent.

Nine were found through the folder hierarchy. Four percent. The structure people
build, the nested folders and the careful tags, was the part nobody used. The
bookmarks that did get used were the ones sitting permanently visible on the
toolbar.

And the finding that should end the argument: bookmarked pages were not re-found
any better than pages the participants had simply visited once and never saved.
The save did nothing.

Before you conclude that people are bad at finding things, they are not. A
separate line of research on personal information management measured people
going back to find something they had seen before, and they succeed 90 to 100
percent of the time, in under a minute, usually with the very first method they
try. Findability is solved. Google solved it.

The failure is not that you cannot find what you saved. It is that you never go
looking.

## Why the click feels so good

Here is the part that took me a while to understand, and it is not a story about
willpower.

When you encounter something that opens a small gap in what you know, that is
not a neutral event. In 2009 Min Jeong Kang and colleagues at Caltech put people
in a scanner and showed them trivia questions. The moment of curiosity, before
any answer arrived, lit up the caudate, a region associated with anticipated
reward. Curiosity registers in the brain the way an expected payoff does.

The same study found people would spend scarce tokens, and wait, to get answers
they were curious about. Curiosity is not a mood. It is a motivational state
with a price attached, and you will pay it.

Kent Berridge's work at Michigan explains the shape of the trap. Berridge spent
decades separating two things that everyone else had collapsed into one word,
reward. There is wanting, driven by dopamine, and there is liking, which is a
different and much smaller system that dopamine barely touches. Wanting is
bigger than liking. You seek more than you are satisfied.

So the sequence goes like this. Something opens a curiosity gap. Your brain
issues a wanting signal. And then, instead of closing the gap by reading the
thing, you perform an action that feels like closing it. You save it. The tension
resolves. The icon fills in.

You got the anticipation without the answer, and your brain filed the whole
episode as handled.

Kang's team also followed up on memory, and this is the cruel part. Higher
curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers one to two weeks later.
Curiosity does make things stick. But only when you actually get the answer.
Saving is the move that reliably prevents that from happening.

## Four things you have heard about this that are wrong

If you go looking for writing about saved-content overload, you will hit the same
four claims everywhere. I chased all four to their sources and they do not hold
up. If you are going to talk about this publicly, do not repeat them.

**"Google is making you forget."** This comes from one 2011 paper in Science by
Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu and Daniel Wegner. Its flagship experiment reported a
121 millisecond effect. The Social Sciences Replication Project ran it again with
234 participants and got 2.89 milliseconds, p = .449. Then a preregistered German
team, deliberately building their study around the original author's own
published objections, tested 117 people and found t(88) = −1.04, p = .301, with a
Bayes factor of 5.07 favouring no effect. Their conclusion was that there was no
conclusive evidence the concept of the internet gets activated at all. It is one
of the most-cited findings in popular psychology and it failed twice.

**The Zeigarnik effect.** The idea that unfinished business stays active in your
memory, which people use to argue that your unread saves are quietly nagging at
you. A 2025 meta-analysis by Ghibellini and Meier pooled the literature. Setting
aside Zeigarnik's own 1927 study, the ratio of interrupted to completed task
recall is 0.99. Interrupted tasks make up 49 percent of what gets recalled.
There is no memory advantage. What does survive is a related finding, the
Ovsiankina effect, that people tend to resume interrupted tasks. Open loops tug
at behaviour. They do not improve memory.

**"Digital amnesia."** This is not a research finding. The term was coined in a
Kaspersky Lab press release on 1 July 2015, based on a survey of a thousand
consumers with no memory test and no control group, and it pivots halfway
through into how many people fail to protect their devices. It is a lead
generation asset for antivirus software that got laundered into the discourse by
repetition.

**"90 percent of saved articles are never read."** No source. Pocket and
Instapaper, the two companies who could have published a read-through rate, never
did. The variants you will see, 70 percent never revisited, 84 percent never
opened, all trace to blog posts citing nothing. There is a similarly popular
claim that X users bookmark 65 million times a day, which turns out to be a
random user's guess that Elon Musk replied to.

Use Bergman's sixteen percent. It is a real experiment and it is worse than most
of the fake numbers anyway.

## What is actually true

The honest version of the offloading story is smaller and more interesting than
the scary version.

Adrian Ward at UT Austin ran eight experiments with 1,917 people and found that
using the internet to answer questions inflates people's estimates of their own
knowledge. Matthew Fisher, Mariel Goddu and Frank Keil found the same across nine
experiments, and their controls are what make it convincing. The inflation was
not general overconfidence, because it disappeared for autobiographical
questions. It was not the information either. The effect persisted when searches
returned no answer at all, and even when they returned zero results. It was the
act of searching that did it. Participants even rated their own brains as more
active when shown fMRI images.

That mechanism has a name from earlier work. Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil
called it the illusion of explanatory depth. Ask people how well they understand
how a zipper works, they rate themselves confidently. Ask them to actually
explain it, step by step, and their self-rating collapses. Access feels like
understanding until the moment you have to produce the understanding.

A folder full of saved posts is a very efficient machine for producing that
illusion. You know roughly what is in there. You know you have something on
that topic. Right up until someone asks you what it said.

By the way, the phrase you will see attached to this, the collector's fallacy, is
not a research construct. It was coined in a blog post by Christian Tietze in
January 2014, which says so in its own text. The intuition is right. The
citation is a blog.

## The finding that keeps everyone honest

Here is what I did not expect, and it is the reason I do not think saving is
simply bad.

Benjamin Storm and Sean Stone ran a study where people studied a list, then
either saved it or closed it, then studied a second list. They remembered the
second list better when the first had been saved. Saving one thing improves your
memory for the next thing, by clearing interference and freeing up capacity. It
replicated at roughly half the original size in an independent study of fifty
people, using paper lists and an actual shredder, so it is not an artifact of
computers.

Storm's own lab has published results in both directions. Saving degrades memory
for the thing saved and improves memory for what comes next.

So offloading is a reallocation, not a deletion. That is genuinely useful. The
problem is not that you save. The problem is that you save and the loop never
closes, so you pay the cost and never collect the benefit.

## What closes the loop

The evidence here is unusually clear, and it says something specific: re-reading
does not work, and retrieving does.

Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke gave students prose passages and had them
either study again or take a recall test. Tested five minutes later, the students
who re-studied did better, 81 percent against 75. Tested a week later, it
reversed hard: 56 percent for the tested group, 42 for the re-studiers. The thing
that felt more productive was worse, and you could only tell after a delay.

Karpicke and Blunt then put retrieval practice against elaborate concept mapping,
the sort of careful organising that a good notes system encourages. One week
later, retrieval scored .67 against .45, an effect size of 1.50. It still won
when the final test was building a concept map, which is the comparison that
should have favoured mapping. Eighty-four percent of students did better after
retrieval. Seventy-five percent predicted the opposite.

That gap between what works and what feels like it works is the whole subject.

In 2013 Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten common study techniques and rated
them. Practice testing and distributed practice came out high utility.
Summarising, highlighting and re-reading came out low. Those bottom three are, of
course, exactly what most people do, and exactly what most saving tools are built
around.

I want to be careful not to oversell this, because the field oversells it
routinely. A 2021 meta-analysis by Yang and colleagues covering 222 studies and
48,478 students in real classrooms found retrieval practice at g = 0.499 overall.
But broken down by comparison: against doing nothing, g = 0.61. Against
re-studying, g = 0.33. Against other active strategies, g = 0.095, which is not
statistically significant.

Retrieval practice is not magic. It reliably beats doing nothing. A saved folder
you never open is doing nothing, which is why it applies here, but the honest
claim is narrow.

## So what do you actually do

Three things follow from all of this, and none of them is "save less."

**Close the loop the same day, on a few things.** Not all of them. The curiosity
spike you felt when you saved something has a short life, and Kang's data says
the memory benefit lands when the answer arrives. Pick two things a day and
finish them.

**Make yourself produce the thing, not re-read it.** Retrieval beats review. In
practice this means answering a question about the material before you look at
it. That is the difference between a system that shows you your highlights again
and a system that asks you what the argument was.

**Attach the return to a cue, not to intent.** This is the most reliable
intervention in behavioural science that almost nobody uses. Peter Gollwitzer and
Paschal Sheeran's meta-analysis of 94 studies found that if-then plans, of the
form "if situation X arises, then I will do Y," move behaviour at d = 0.65.
Compare that to the gap they were solving: a medium-to-large change in
intention produces only a small-to-medium change in actual behaviour. Deciding to
read your saves does close to nothing. "When I make coffee, I open one saved
note" does a surprising amount.

## The thing I keep coming back to

Bergman's saddest result is not the sixteen percent. It is that bookmarked pages
were re-found no better than pages people had merely visited.

Which means the save button, functionally, does nothing to your future. It is not
a memory and it is not a plan. It is a way of feeling the click of having caught
something, at the exact moment when the honest options were to read it now or to
let it go.

Go open the bottom of your saved folder. Not to process it. Just to see what a
year of good intentions actually looks like when you scroll it.
